# Thesis 5 — Steel & Iron Ore: What Is Actually Sorting the Field?

**Date:** 2026-08-12 (filings through Q2 2026, filed June 23 – Aug 12 2026)
**Question tested:** SLX +11.1%/21d, XME +15.4%/21d, and our Steel/Iron Ore field +10.4%/21d with **dispersion at the 88th percentile**. A uniform commodity lift would not disperse. What is separating the winners from the losers, and which companies are genuinely positioned to capture it?

---

## What is actually sorting this field

**The axis is: do you MELT metal and sell it into a market your own government has walled off with import barriers — and does your pricing mechanism let the move reach earnings this quarter?**

It is one axis with two clauses, and it is *not* "US tariffs," *not* "EAF versus blast furnace," and *not* "steel is going up." Each of those fails a test:

**Test 1 — Is it US tariffs specifically? No. It is home-market trade protection, wherever you melt.** Every single company with expanding margins names an import barrier *in its own jurisdiction*, in its own words:

- **US** — Nucor's 10-Q, filed today: *"Federal trade policies, including anti-dumping and countervailing duty laws in combination with Section 232 national security tariffs, are continuing to reduce the volume of unfairly traded imports into the United States."* ([NUE 10-Q](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/73309/000119312526345891/nue-20260704.htm)). Steel Dynamics: strength *"supported by domestic trade actions, manufacturing reshoring, infrastructure program funding, and the increasing regionalization of supply chains within the United States"* ([STLD 8-K EX-99.1](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1022671/000110465926085277/tm2620920d1_ex99-1.htm)).
- **Mexico/Brazil** — Ternium: EBITDA +50% q/q *"amid the implementation of more effective measures against unfairly traded steel imports"* in Mexico, plus *"the measures adopted by the Brazilian government to promote fair competition"*; and forward, *"the substitution of Asian imported steel at several OEMs"* ([TX 6-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1342874/000134287426000044/prternium2q2026.htm)).
- **Europe** — ArcelorMittal: *"Activity levels have improved following the implementation of the TRQ measures, with stronger order books supporting capacity restarts across Europe,"* with European Q3 shipments guided *"stable to slightly higher... compared with the typical high-single-digit seasonal decline"* ([MT 6-K EX-99.1](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1243429/000124342926000072/a2q26earningsrelease.htm)). CMC independently corroborates: the EU CBAM *"has strengthened domestic demand, with third quarter total steel shipments increasing 41.2% sequentially"* ([CMC EX-99.1](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/22444/000002244426000038/cmc-5312026xearningsreleas.htm)).

Three continents, three different trade instruments, same result. The unifying mechanism is not one government's policy — it is the **partitioning of a globally oversupplied steel market into protected regional pockets**. Note that Nucor's own risk factors still list *"excess world capacity for steel production"*. There is no global shortage. There are walls.

**Test 2 — Is it EAF versus blast furnace? No.** EAF names (NUE +17%, STLD +13%, CMC +13%) *and* blast-furnace names (MT +12%, TX +22%) are both up, while a pure blast-furnace supplier (SXC +14%) is up for unrelated reasons. Furnace route is not the sort. What EAF *does* provide is the **speed** of spread capture, which is the second clause of the axis.

**Test 3 — The measurable expression of the axis is the METAL SPREAD (realized price/ton minus raw-material cost/ton) and its direction.** This is the one number that cleanly ranks the field:

The winners are the companies where **output price is rising faster than input cost**. Nucor's realized price rose $71/ton sequentially against $18/ton of scrap; Steel Dynamics' rose $105 against $16.

**Test 4 — Speed of pass-through decides who books it this quarter.** Four distinct pricing mechanisms exist in this field, and they sort the names as sharply as the trade barriers do:

1. **Spot / weekly reset** — Nucor's published consumer spot price for hot-rolled coil went $1,045/st (April 13) → $1,145/st (July 27) → $1,155/st (Aug 3) → $1,160/st (Aug 10), a **+$115/st (+11%) ladder in four months** ([Steel Market Update](https://www.steelmarketupdate.com/2026/08/03/nucor-ups-spot-hr-price-by-10-ton-again/)). NUE, STLD and FRD price off this.
2. **Surcharge / index contracts** — pass raw material through but *muffle the spread*. CRS surcharge revenue was **$171.3M of $851.0M net sales (20.1%)**; MTUS surcharges were **$457 of $1,958 net sales per ton (23%)**; ATI states *"the majority of our products are sold utilizing raw material surcharges and index mechanisms."*
3. **Long-term take-or-pay with cost pass-through** — literally zero price exposure. SXC's coke is sold *"under long-term, take-or-pay contracts"* with *"the pass-through of lower coal prices."*
4. **Buy-side / fabrication** — the inverted sign. Higher steel is a *cost*.

**Test 5 — End-market mix is the secondary sort, and it is real.** The demand named by the winners is remarkably consistent and it is **not automotive and not residential**: STLD's fabrication backlog is *"nearly 45 percent higher than a year ago and extending into the first quarter 2027,"* driven by *"commercial construction, data centers, manufacturing, warehousing, and healthcare."* CMC cites *"mega-projects investments across data centers, semiconductors, and ongoing energy-related build outs."* Nucor's **plate shipments rose 17% q/q and 25% y/y** and tubular **+39% y/y** — the products that go into data centers, transformers and grid. Conversely, the auto- and resi-levered names are where the damage is: MTUS's automotive base price per ton **fell 6.0% y/y** on 43% of its volume; Worthington Steel took a **$94.5M impairment** on electrical steel from *"weakened demand... particularly industrial motors... due to increased foreign competition, and in automotive, some delayed program launches"*; Gibraltar is **83% residential** in a *"flat-to-down market."*

**The critical structural finding: 9 of these 18 companies do not melt steel.** SXC makes coke. ROCK, IIIN, NWPX, WS, WOR and FRD buy steel and convert or distribute it. TWI makes off-highway tires and wheels. TS buys steel for pipe. For six of these, a rising steel price is a **cost**, an inverted sign — and their own filings say so. This is not inference; it is verified below, including from *inside* the steelmakers themselves.

**The cleanest single proof of the inversion comes from Steel Dynamics' own income statement.** In the same quarter that its steel segment's operating income rose 30% on spread expansion, its **steel fabrication segment's operating income fell** ($85M vs $90M sequentially; $174M vs $210M for the half) — explicitly *"due to a decrease in average pricing of $100 per ton combined with higher steel raw material input costs of $95 per ton."* One company, one quarter: the melting side up 30%, the fabricating side down. That is the axis, measured inside a single set of books.

**One genuinely separate axis is also present, and it is being confused with the first.** ATI and CRS are nickel/titanium/cobalt alloy producers with **zero carbon-steel and zero Section 232 exposure**. ATI's +25%/21d is an aerospace-supply-scarcity story (record $4.4B backlog, +18% y/y, *"demand... continues to outpace available supply"*), not a steel story. Nothing in either company's filings attributes anything to steel prices or trade policy. Their presence inflates the field's measured dispersion — two unrelated drivers inside one SIC bucket.

## Method + caveats

**Sources.** Every hard number above is taken from a primary SEC filing retrieved from EDGAR on 2026-08-12 — 10-Qs, 10-Ks, and 8-K/6-K earnings exhibits, all linked inline. Ticker-to-CIK mapping came from `https://www.sec.gov/files/company_tickers.json`; filing indices from `https://data.sec.gov/submissions/CIK{10-digit}.json`. Documents were fetched by `curl` through the agent proxy with a descriptive User-Agent and parsed locally.

**Foreign private issuers — no 10-Q exists.** Four names file **6-K/20-F rather than 10-Q/10-K**, and their disclosure is materially thinner: **TX** (Ternium, Luxembourg), **MT** (ArcelorMittal, Luxembourg), **PKX** (POSCO Holdings, Korea) and **TS** (Tenaris, Luxembourg). TX and MT nevertheless publish full quarterly price-per-ton and shipment tables, so they are verified to the same standard as the US names. **TS** publishes volumes and margins but no realized price per ton. **PKX is the weakest link in this review**: its 6-K is a *provisional, unaudited* consolidated summary with no volume, price, spread or segment data whatsoever — its 26% score rests on a two-line income summary plus 20-F export data and should be treated as **LOW confidence**.

**Stale reporting periods — flagged explicitly.** Three companies have fiscal periods ending **May 31, 2026**, which is *before* the June–August price ladder that this thesis is about: **CMC** (fiscal Q3), **WS** (fiscal year-end) and **WOR** (fiscal year-end). Their reported numbers cannot confirm or deny capture of the current move. **CRS** (fiscal Q4) and **FRD** (fiscal Q1) have June 30 period-ends and do capture part of it; the eleven calendar-quarter reporters run to late June or early July. This calendar effect is a real and under-appreciated driver of the 21d-versus-63d divergence, most visibly in CMC (+13%/21d but +1%/63d).

**Explicitly LOW-confidence items.**
- **PKX** — no per-ton, volume, or segment data available in any SEC filing; the −36%/63d move is unexplained by the filings and may be driven by the battery-materials segment rather than steel.
- **NWPX** — the release and guidance are unambiguously positive, yet the stock is −16%/21d. I could not identify a filed disclosure that reconciles the two; something outside the primary filings is driving that reaction.
- **CRS** — same problem in reverse: record results, raised targets, −5%/21d. My explanation (realisation per pound down 11%, medical −30%, PEP margin halved, story already priced at +25%/63d) is supported by the filing but the *reaction* is inferred, not verified.
- The claim that Nucor raised its spot price in a long unbroken run of consecutive weeks appears in trade press; I have verified only the specific quoted prices ($1,045 on Apr 13, $1,145 on Jul 27, $1,155 on Aug 3, $1,160 on Aug 10) from Steel Market Update. The +$115/st ladder is solid; the exact streak length is **LOW confidence**.
- Metal-spread figures for NUE and STLD are **arithmetic derived** from separately disclosed price and raw-material lines; neither company reports "spread" as such. CMC and MT *do* report metal margin / EBITDA per tonne directly.

**Companies that do not belong in this field.** In descending order of confidence: **TWI** (off-highway tyres and wheels; legacy SIC 3312 — recommend excluding from field statistics), **WOR** (consumer and building products), **SXC** (coke on take-or-pay contracts with no steel-price exposure in either direction), **ROCK** (83% residential building products), **TS** (OCTG, driven by drilling and Hormuz). **ATI** and **CRS** are legitimate metal producers but sit on a **separate axis** (aerospace/defence alloy scarcity, nickel/titanium/cobalt raws, surcharge pass-through) with zero carbon-steel or Section 232 exposure — they should be analysed as a sub-group, not pooled with the mills.

**Companies with confirmed inverted sign (higher steel = higher cost).** **IIIN** (buys wire rod; CEO states input costs outpaced selling prices; gross margin 10.2% vs 17.1%), **ROCK** (price actions to offset commodity inflation), **NWPX** (buys plate/coil for water pipe), **WS** (processor; spread compressed, benefit is transient inventory holding gains), **WOR**, **TWI**. **FRD** is the special case: structurally a buyer, but currently monetising the *rate of change* of HRC through inventory timing — positive while prices climb, self-liquidating when they plateau. Both **NUE** and **STLD** carry the same inversion internally in their fabrication segments; STLD's is large enough to be visible in reported segment income.

**What would falsify this thesis.** If scrap prices catch up to finished-steel prices over the next two quarters, the spread — which is the entire mechanism — compresses regardless of tariffs, and the top four ranks collapse toward the middle. Watch Nucor's disclosed scrap cost per gross ton against its realized price per ton; Q2's $18 versus $71 sequential move is the number to track. Because the price wall is built on proclamation rather than physical scarcity — Nucor's own risk factors still name *"excess world capacity for steel production"* — this is a policy-reversible setup, not a supply-reversible one.
